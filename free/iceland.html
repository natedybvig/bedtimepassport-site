NIGHT 1 · ICELAND

The Land Where the Ground Breathes

A free story from Book 1 of Bedtime Passport. About six minutes, for ages 4 to 8.

For the grown-up: pronunciations and quick guide
Capital
Reykjavik [RAY-kya-veek] (means “Smoky Bay”)
Key pronunciations
Althing [AHL-thing], one of the world’s oldest parliaments; Thingvellir [THING-vet-lir], where it met; tölt [TURLT], the Icelandic horse’s smooth fifth gait; Jólabókaflóð [YOH-la-boh-ka-floth], the Christmas Book Flood; Góða nótt [GOH-tha NOHT], good night.
Historical anchor
874 AD (first permanent Norse settlement by Ingólfur Arnarson) and 930 AD (the first Althing).
Reading pace
Calm and unhurried, about 6 minutes.
Five quick facts about Iceland
  • An Old, Old Parliament: In 930 AD, Icelandic families gathered at Thingvellir to make laws together. Their Althing still meets today, and it is one of the oldest parliaments in the world.
  • Power from the Earth: Hot water from deep underground warms about nine out of ten Icelandic homes, and nearly all of the country’s electricity comes from hot earth and rushing rivers.
  • Pure Viking Horses: Icelandic horses came with the Vikings over 1,000 years ago, and no other horses have been allowed in since. They can do a super-smooth fifth gait called the tölt.
  • Brand-New Mosquitoes (and No Moose): For as long as anyone knew, Iceland was one of the only places on Earth with no mosquitoes. Then, in October 2025, a few were found there for the very first time, and scientists are now watching to see if they stay. Iceland has no wild moose, either.
  • The Christmas Book Flood: On Christmas Eve, Icelanders give each other books, then read in bed while eating chocolate.

Week 1, The Arctic Rim. Tonight is stop number 1.

When Mossy says “Goggles on!”, make circles with your fingers over your eyes together. At the end, whisper the good night in Icelandic.

There is a moose at the end of your bed.

He is your moose. Soft and brown, about the size of a pillow, with big, kind eyes. But tonight he is wearing something new: an old leather flying helmet, with two holes cut in the top for his antlers, a pair of round goggles pushed up on his forehead, and a white pilot’s scarf around his neck. Under one hoof, he holds a small blue book.

“Evening, explorer!” says the moose, in a deep, rumbly voice. “I’m Mossy. Captain Mossy, if you like. This is your Bedtime Passport. It only works when you’re sleepy. And every night, it will fly us somewhere amazing.”

“Fly?” you whisper. “But moose can’t fly.”

Mossy peeks at you over his goggles. “Moose,” he says, “can do all sorts of things when nobody is watching. When I say the magic words, pull your goggles down. Like this.” He holds two hoof-circles over his eyes. “Ready? Goggles on!”

You make circles with your fingers and hold them over your eyes. The Passport flashes blue. And Mossy grows. And grows. And GROWS, until he is as big as a pony, and you are sitting on his warm, wide back, holding his antlers like handlebars.

WHOOOOSH! Your bedroom folds up like a paper map, and you are flying.

When his hooves touch down, the ground is warm.

Warm! You slide off, and the black rock under your toes feels as toasty as a sleeping cat. All around, white steam curls up out of cracks in the ground. Fffssshhh. Fffssshhh. It looks as if the whole world is breathing out.

“Welcome to Iceland,” says Mossy, pushing up his goggles. “Long ago, a Viking sailed here in a wooden ship. He saw all this steam and thought it was smoke! So he named the place Reykjavik [RAY-kya-veek]. It means ‘Smoky Bay.’”

“Was it smoke?” you ask.

“Nope!” Mossy chuckles, a rumbly chuckle you can feel in your tummy. “It’s heat from deep inside the Earth. Iceland is full of volcanoes and geysers that go... oh dear. Stand back!”

KER-SPLOOOOSH! A fountain of hot water shoots up taller than a house. Mossy tries to hide behind a rock. The rock is much smaller than Mossy. His whole bottom sticks out, and the water rains down on it. You laugh until you snort.

“Here’s the clever part,” says Mossy, wiping his goggles. “Icelanders use all that heat. Pipes carry hot water from underground to warm houses and even outdoor swimming pools. You can swim outside in the snow, snug as a bath!”

Something shaggy trots out of the mist. It is a small, sturdy horse with a mane like a tangled curtain. She stops. She stares at Mossy.

“She’s never seen a moose before,” Mossy whispers. “There are no moose in Iceland. Well. There is one now.”

The horse sniffs Mossy’s nose. Mossy sniffs back. Friends!

You climb onto the horse, and she sets off, so smooth you hardly bounce at all. “Icelandic horses have a special way of walking called the tölt [TURLT],” says Mossy, flying low beside you. “It’s so smooth you could carry a cup of cocoa and not spill a drop. Their great-great-great-grandparents came here with the Vikings, more than a thousand years ago.”

The horse carries you to a wide, grassy valley between tall rock walls. It is called Thingvellir [THING-vet-lir]. “Long ago, families met here every summer to make rules together,” Mossy says softly. “They called it the Althing [AHL-thing]. It still meets today. It’s one of the oldest parliaments in the world. A parliament is a group of people who make a country’s rules.”

Then Mossy tilts one big ear. Then the other. “Listen. Do you hear anything missing?”

You listen. Wind. Water. The horse breathing. Hardly any buzzing at all.

“For a very long time, Iceland had no mosquitoes,” says Mossy. “Not one! Then, not long ago, someone spotted a few, for the very first time.” He wiggles his nose. “Back home, mosquitoes tickle my nose all summer. I hope these ones stay very small.”

The sky turns the color of blueberries. You come to a tiny house with a roof made of real grass. Mossy squeezes through the door, antlers first. Inside, books fill the shelves from floor to ceiling.

“On Christmas Eve,” Mossy says, and his voice is slow and yawny now, “Icelanders give each other books. Then they read in bed and eat chocolate. It’s called the Christmas Book Flood.”

“That sounds like the best night ever,” you murmur.

“It’s a tie with tonight,” says Mossy.

You snuggle under a heavy wool blanket. Through the window, green ribbons of light swish slowly across the sky, like a scarf made of stars. The Northern Lights. Under the house, the warm ground hums. The horse is asleep in her field. Mossy shrinks back to pillow size, curls up at the end of your bed, and pulls his goggles down over his sleepy eyes.

“Whisper it with me,” he says. “Góða nótt [GOH-tha NOHT].”

“Góða nótt,” you whisper.

Goggles off.

Dream Seed (read slowly as the lights go out)

Close your eyes and lie very still under the Northern Lights. Watch the green ribbons swish across the sky, slower and slower, softer and softer, while the warm ground hums beneath you.
